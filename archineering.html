What was the course about?

Archineering, a blend of architecture and engineering, captures the essence of this minor, an approach rooted in designing through making, testing, and hands-on exploration. Rather than starting from theory alone, the course emphasized prototyping, iteration, and circular design, encouraging us to understand materials, construction, and design logic through physical experimentation.

From the very first lesson, we were pushed to think fast and build even faster. We began with a simple challenge: sketch a design in 30 minutes, then prototype it using whatever was at hand: paper, cardboard, plastic cups, straws. The goal wasn't perfection, but learning by doing, testing ideas in their simplest form. Another exercise had us deconstruct existing architecture, analyzing houses by breaking them down into basic elements like volume, grounding, and construction. These were reduced into a set of icons, which we then used to create entirely new designs. This method helped us explore modularity and adaptability, encouraging multiple iterations from the same base elements and testing what worked and what didn’t.





This icon-based method fed directly into our first assignment: designing a tiny house using these icon combinations. It wasn’t about finding one solution, but generating many, adapting and evolving the design with each step.

In the second assignment, we partnered with The New Makers, working on the digital fabrication of a chair made from a single sheet of plywood. This experience deepened our understanding of material efficiency, fabrication techniques, and full scale prototyping. This assignment was where I first discovered my interest in the combination of digital fabrication and woodworking.

The final assignment focused on climate-responsive design, challenging us to develop a climate-adaptive tiny house. This tied together everything we had learned: from conceptual design and iconography, to construction systems, prototyping, and sustainability.

Each project throughout the minor emphasized making as a thinking tool, with circular design principles and adaptive experimentation at the core. Archineering, as a methodology, blurred the lines between engineering precision and architectural imagination, bridging the gap through physical, testable outcomes.

Assignment 1: Tiny house

The first assignment was simple, you had to design a tiny house in a 7x7x7 meter volume, using a set of symbols as discussed in the introduction to document your iterations and lead the design. What was quite refreshing was the fact that there were no other constraints at first, you started out by experimenting with geometry to later adapt the design to the context that was given. The focus on fast prototyping, sketching and iteration, not the final product but the process along the way.

I did this mostly by using Rhino, a 3D modeling software to quickly generate a couple of options for the design and be able to document each change but I also made a quick physical model from some trashbag ties to see what orientation of the different geometries worked best. And over the course of the assignment a lot of changes were made, all to benefit the design.

I primarily used Rhino, a 3D modeling software, to quickly generate multiple design options and efficiently document each iteration. To complement the digital process, I also built a simple physical model using trash bag ties, which helped me evaluate how different geometries and orientations worked in three-dimensional space. Throughout the assignment, numerous adjustments were made, each aimed at improving the design’s functionality and form. For example trying to with such a small plot find space for stairs to create more floor space, tying out different options until finally falling onto a spiraling staircase.

At last the site context was introduced, now the plot was only 6x7x7 in Voorburg, nestled tightly between neighboring houses with a open side facing the road and aonther on the side instead of to the back and front, so the design had to be adapted accordingly. Changes included narrowing the width, refining material choices to fit the surroundings, reorienting openings, and reconsidering access points. Since the plot was enclosed on most sides and lacked a garden, the design featured a single front entrance with no rear doors, responding directly to the site conditions.









Working through these iterations and site-driven adaptations, I developed a design that thoughtfully balanced form, function, and context. I also made a more detialed physical model to better understand how the interior spaces would feel and relate to one another, which helped inform further adjustments. While the final design was a strong starting point, it clearly left plenty of room for improvement—understandable given the short timeframe of the assignment. For example a more open facade on the side instead of focussing on the back and front. Still, this process felt rewarding, capturing the spirit of rapid iteration and hands-on exploration that defined the course and setting a solid foundation for future development.

Assignment 2: CNC chair

As mentioned in the introduction the second assignment was to design ‘something to sit on’ from a single piece of plywood (18 x 1220 x 1220 mm) that could be fully assembled without the use of any screws and bolts, relying solely on friction-fit connections. The challenge was to create something sturdy enough to support a person while maximizing material efficiency.

We worked closely together with The New Makers who have designed their own modular prefab housing units using CNC technology. They also have their own furniture brand called fabrikoos who just like the assignment create furniture with fiction fit connections. They helped us through the process of going from a design to a 1:1 prototype by providing us with their large CNC-routers and advice on how to best go about designing for something like this through digital fabrication. This experience sparked my passion for CNC machining and digital fabrication, especially working with wood, and deepened my interest in digital and modular architecture.

For my own design, I again used Rhino to explore many design iterations but also created a few physical models from a few scraps of cardboard to get a better idea of what I wanted as well as sketched some ideas and reference a few other similar projects. Eventually I stumbled upon a form I liked for the chair but realized that cutting it out of one slice of plywood was not going to happen as I used too much material as well as the fact that the connections were quite hard to make as they were angled. So from there I changed the approach to instead create a design for a chair that reused the cut-out shapes for the chair and used simpler connections.
I then tested my design with a smaller cardboard model to verify if the chair could hold enough weight or if it would instantly collapse, and when it held up the way I wanted it to, I prepared detailed CNC-ready technical drawings, learning about drill sizes, tolerances, and digital fabrication workflows for the first time. The New Makers then produced the full-scale prototype from plywood and we assembled them at the faculty where we presented our designs.






Of course this was just the first prototype, so there’s definitely room for improvement. For example, for example I could’ve rounded off the edges at the ends as those revealed to be quite sharp. The seat was also a bit too high, as I based the dimensions on my own measurements, making it comfortable but not necessarily a lounge chair like I had envisioned. But that is what prototypes are for to see if what you designed on paper or digitally actually works in reality like you thought or if there is something you missed. And despite these issues, I really love the chair’s form and design. I used it in my room for quite a while, and now that I’ve moved to a smaller space, it’s disassembled and tucked away in a corner. Its ability to be easily taken apart and stored flat makes it perfect for saving space until I have room again.

Assignment 3: Climate adaptive tiny house

For the third assignment, I designed a tiny house that adapts to the changing climate and weather conditions of a specific location. The focus was on creating an innovative feature that maintains a comfortable indoor environment year-round. I chose Osaka, Japan, as the site due to its distinct seasonal variations: cold, dry winters and hot, humid summers, which presented an interesting design challenge. (Image from Meteoblue)



My inspiration came from several sources. Primarily, I looked at the concept of evaporative cooling, exemplified by the “Cool Bricks” project by Virginia San Fratello and Ronald Rael. This method lowers air temperature by adding water vapor. Historically, before refrigeration, people used porous ceramic vessels that cooled water through evaporation. Ancient frescoes from around 2500 BC even depict servants fanning water jars to cool rooms. (Image from Emergingobjects)



In my design, I incorporated two humid porous facades on opposite sides of the house to recreate this cooling effect. By utilizing cross-ventilation and capturing prevailing winds, the facades cool the building naturally. The small perforations in the facade allow daylight to filter in without direct sunlight, and these panels can be sealed during winter months to retain warmth. However, considering Japan’s high summer humidity, I recognized that adding moisture inside might not always be ideal.








To address this, I conducted a simple experiment with a wet porous facade to test its cooling effectiveness against a dry facade both with different sized holes and impact on indoor humidity. While the temperature dropped noticeably, humidity rose significantly. To mitigate this, I explored using a breathable membrane (Temish for example) within the facade holes. This membrane allows airflow but blocks moisture and insects, enhancing comfort and usability.






Additionally, I drew inspiration from traditional Japanese architecture, particularly the glazed tiled double roofs with overhanging ridges. This design keeps summer sun out when it’s high in the sky but allows low winter sunlight to warm the interior. The reflective glazed tiles further reduce heat gain. The angled roof promotes natural ventilation by allowing warm air to rise and exit through adjustable upper panels.

Side windows provide daylight and passive solar heating in winter by opening their protective panels, while closing them in summer to optimize the cooling effect of the porous facades. This combination creates a house that fully adapts to Osaka’s seasonal climate, maintaining comfort throughout the year.
