Homecraft

Master thesis | 2024 - 2025

Making shelter design and construction accessible to non-experts through CNC-aided design and Makerspaces.


Learn more

What was the project about?

The focus of my thesis originated from an earlier project developed together with Amir Ghadiri during CORE (Computation for Earthquake Resilience and Recovery), where we explored ways to improve shelter design in response to disasters such as earthquakes. Following the encouragement of our professor, Dr. S. (Serdar) Așut, we both decided to continue with the subject for our theses, each taking a different direction. Where my focus was on exploring the viability to make shelter design and construction accessible to non-experts through CNC-aided design and Makerspaces.

As natural and man-made disasters displace millions of people worldwide every year, there is an urgent need for shelters that are not only safe and habitable but also adaptable to the diverse cultural and environmental needs of displaced communities and innovation is always appreciated to be able to make this process more efficient.

So, recognizing the broader challenges faced by displaced communities, such as the need for rapid deployment, cultural adaptability, sustainability, and community empowerment, the main objective of my thesis was to see if with the help of computational design and digital fabrication it is possible to let individuals with limited expertise in architecture, engineering, and design, to design and actualize personalized shelter solutions within their own community. Using the resources that are locally available without the need for expert involvement.

To explore how technologies like CNC machines, combined with accessible materials such as plywood, could democratize shelter construction, making it feasible for non-experts to create sustainable, functional and fitting housing solutions rooted in their own culture and environment for a smaller price. Also empowering individuals to take an active role in the creation of their homes, which supports community recovery, autonomy, and resilience. Ultimately, hoping to contribute to a larger conversation around self-reliance, accessibility, and sustainability in the built environment, emphasizing the transformative potential of community-driven spaces and open-source design.

The main idea

The proposal for this project was to create an online environment where individuals can design their own customizable shelters, adapted to local materials, cultural traditions, and specific needs. The system guides users through the design process while ensuring the structural integrity and resilience of each iteration by computational means, making sure the shelter remains safe and habitable without restricting user creativity. Once the design is completed, users are provided with the tools to manufacture each custom element locally through digital fabrication technologies like CNC-routing, fostering a strong sense of ownership, empowerment, and self-reliance within their communities. After production, the shelter can be easily transported to the site, assembled within a few days, and, if necessary, deconstructed and relocated, upgraded, repaired and or reused (see the diagram down below for an overview).



This resulted in three concepts coming together into a single cohesive idea: the architectural concept (geometry, layout, materiality), the structural concept (supporting structure, loads, connections), and the digital concept (parametric model, user interface, database).

Architectural concept

For the architectural concept a few main key points were taken into consideration, among which modularity and expandability, customizable layouts, use of local materials, sustainability, longevity, ease of assembly as well as deconstruction, natural ventilation and daylight, thermal performance and cultural aesthetic flexibility. Which resulted in the following design:

A simple geometry, universally recognizable as the shape of a house, with a rectangular or square layout and a gabled roof. Which might seem simple but does give the opportunity to customize layouts, adjust dimensions and change materials based on availability with ease. Also supporting phased growth, allowing extensions horizontally or vertically as circumstances change.

More details about how this is translated to the structure will be discussed in the structural concept.

The layout of the shelter is based on a flexible grid system, allowing users to customize the internal setup according to their needs. While the position of columns, the bathroom, and the outdoor area are fixed, users can adapt the floorplan’s size (rectangular or square), define door and window placements (avoiding bracing zones), and choose additional partitioning. The inclusion of a kitchen is optional to respect cultural differences. The system accommodates different living situations, such as wheelchair accessibility or multi-story expansions, and even within a single base structure, multiple interior layouts are possible depending on user preferences and needs.



As for the material selection, the idea is to prioritize local availability, sustainability, cost-efficiency, and minimal disruption to the local economy. An online database helps users choose suitable materials based on their location, offering detailed information to support informed decisions. Users can either gather the necessary materials themselves or place an order directly through the platform.



For the structure, standardized templates use composite wood materials like OSB (more affordable) or Plywood (higher performance), both WBP-glued and FSC-certified for outdoor use. Outer walls and roofs are protected with an additional weatherproof layer, and insulation is optional depending on climate and budget. All joints are friction-fit without adhesives, allowing easy assembly, disassembly, and repair. Interior walls are finished with materials like gypsum for fire protection, while some partitions use just a single plywood sheet to save space and resources.



Climate-responsive design principles like passive ventilation, thermal insulation, and raised foundations, different roof angles and overhang, ensure adaptability across different environments.

Structural concept

The structure is based on a modular system of portal frames, arranged on a grid generated based on the layout the user creates and the results from the digital model which determines the placement of frames and their connective elements as well as their dimensions. More about this process will be discussed in the Digital concept.

The decision introduce a middle column in the space might seem like it takes away from the usable space inside however it does enhance the structural stability and allows users to fully utilize the open space if desired or easily partition it to suit their specific needs and create some privacy.



The frames support the roof, walls, and floors through a series of friction-fit joints, ensuring stability while allowing full deconstruction and expansion. To create lateral stability, at least two-fifths of each side is braced, with flexible bracing positions to allow for window placement.




The connections use a variety of joints optimized for different forces: key joints, dowels, finger tenons, lapped dovetails, and snap fits. These allow the structure to be assembled without adhesives, making it strong, repairable, and adaptable over time.

The frames are connected by ribs, secured with snap-fit joints that can be locked or unlocked with slight pressure, making use of the natural flexibility of the wood.
